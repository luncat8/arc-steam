# Arc Steam

A side-view energy-driven mining simulation. The current prototype combines a passive ball, a force-limited platform that rides a smoothed rubble heightmap, collector/reflector regions, a tilting sprung reflector piston, limited bounce pulses (the bounce drive is on by default), explicit energy accounting, a two-dimensional mineral rock field with a smooth heightmap surface (or optional square cells), falling debris, a debris Y-compression setting that re-lays the floor tiles and the ground they present, a local road grader, mass-bearing cargo, wall markets, thermal management, a prerequisite technology tree, purchasable equipment gated by research, a researched ballistic computer overlay, a centre ground charge pad with an emergency credit resupply, test mode that grants every player all research and equipment, a four-duty autopilot that intercepts, keeps the drive and levels the bounce, competitor platforms that work the same floor and independently fund their own research and machinery, a player scoreboard that hands any platform its controls or its policy, a ball arrangement of one shared, two shared or one ball per platform, a system-bar ground-bounce setting, a multi-flight ballistic computer overlay with a two-bounce horizon, and telemetry warnings that highlight an empty reserve, a full cargo or overheating. Together they close the mine–collect–sell–upgrade loop. Wear, crew experience, and later energy eras remain open milestones.

## Run

Open `index.html` directly in a modern browser. No build, server, dependencies, or internet connection is required.

Optional local HTTP preview:

```sh
python3 -m http.server 8000 --bind 0.0.0.0
```

## Layout

A status bar is fixed to the foot of the viewport. Its left side keeps the three states a player watches — reserve, cargo and machinery temperature, each turning amber and then red at the telemetry thresholds — and its right side carries the messages that only exist while their condition does: the ball's state (orange while it lies on the ground, waiting to be collected) and, while the reserve is low and the platform is on the ground, the two ways out of it (red: park on the centre pad, or click low ground to travel). Standing on the pad or refilling the reserve ends the message, and neither hint lives in a panel any more.

The system bar has two rows: the first holds New game and the world setup — rock shape, ball arrangement and ground bounce — and the second holds the run readouts and modes: clock, pause, speed, Communism and test-mode switches. Below it, full-width telemetry and the PLAYERS strip follow: one line per platform with its colour, money, a `BOT` switch and the whole line as the control switch. The main workspace uses the full browser width, with cargo/storage to the left of the chamber and platform controls to its right. The desktop canvas is capped at 1280 × 748.5 CSS px (smaller in short windows); extra width expands cargo instead of the canvas, removing horizontal table scrolling when space permits. Rock-colour modes remain visible beneath the chamber. The CARGO STORAGE telemetry card opens MARKET / LEDGER; FUNDS / SOLD opens SCIENCE + SHOP, a single visual progression tree where a base-chassis root bar (`KIT` free ramp toggle, `BUY` road grader, `BAY` engine powertrain selector, `BOT` commercial garage grader) branches along SVG prerequisite edges into ten combined node cards (`SCI` research on top, unlocked `↳ BUY` equipment or tier selector directly beneath) alongside the live platform specification. The tree, available funds and specification follow the selected player, so a purchase never spends another player's purse. Mineral details appear as a tooltip while the pointer is over visible rock; the cargo column moves below the chamber and the progression tree stacks into a mobile sheet on narrow screens.

Explanations live in one context-help drawer instead of in the panels, so every panel keeps a single short status line. Each section and panel title carries a `?` mark: hovering one with a precise pointer previews its topic without taking focus, and clicking pins it. Touch has no hover, so there a tap opens the drawer and a second tap closes it, and pressing and holding any panel for half a second opens that panel's topic without hunting for a mark — the drawer then arrives as a bottom sheet that a downward drag on its head dismisses. `H` (or `F1`) toggles the drawer from anywhere and reopens the last topic read, `Escape` closes it, and the topic index at its foot switches between all available topics. Content is data in `js/help.js`, so a topic is text and key rows rather than markup.

Ground impact loses all ball energy at the default `GROUND` setting. Move the platform over a grounded ball to recover it. Every platform has the same deck: the amber collector catches and holds balls, the green reflector bounces them off its own tilt, and the solid hull under the two faces stops a ball that reaches the chassis. Release transfers a stored ball onto the reflector and pays for lifting it. A 42 J pulse at 80% efficiency debits up to 52.5 J from the shared reserve, and the tuning is sized so the third powered bounce from a release reaches the ceiling.

`GROUND` in the system bar is a sandbox chamber rule rather than an upgrade, like the rock shape and the ball arrangement: it stays chosen across New game. `Lose all` is the classic game above. `Lose 50%` keeps half of the ball's normal impact energy, so it leaves the rubble with 71% of its arriving speed and keeps bouncing, smaller each time; `Lose none` is elastic. Only the normal component is damped, the lost share becomes heat for a ball in the rig's ledger, and a rebound too small to see settles the ball instead of buzzing a millimetre above the floor. The ballistic computer uses the same rule, so a drawn flight follows the real rebound. The energy balance stays exact in every mode.

Communism mode is a switch in the system bar, or the `M` key. The commons owns the machinery, so every price in the game drops to zero: research and equipment are free, the emergency resupply is free, and the technology tree stops gating anything — every node and every machine is available on the first click. The prices are the only thing that changes; the measured effects still apply, so a free drive coil still adds 12 J of pulse and a free chassis still cuts 12 kg of dry mass, and the energy ledger stays exact. The panels follow the mode: row titles read `FREE` instead of a price, the funds card reads `commons` (sales are still measured and still recorded), the market shows a free import, and the ledger books each handout as `free` rather than as a credit line. It is a mode rather than world state, so a new game keeps it, and switching it off puts every price and every gate back.

Test mode sits beside Communism mode in the system bar. Enabling it grants every research node and installs every equipment item for every player immediately, bypassing both funds and prerequisites while applying the normal measured effects and mass changes. It is separate from Communism mode: test grants do not spend credits. Switching test mode off does not revoke grants; they remain active until **New game**, and the mode stays selected across that reset.

The reflector is a sprung piston. Its face tilts ±10° around its centre, so reflections and powered pushes leave along the tilted face normal instead of always going straight up; the ballistic computer predicts deck contacts against the same tilted plane. Each contact compresses the piston along its normal in proportion to the normal impact speed (0.024 m per m/s, capped at 0.16 m), and a damped spring (650 N/m, damping 18) extends it again as the visible push, overshooting slightly before it settles in about half a second. Compression is a rendered state only — the collision plane stays at the rest position, so animation never feeds back into the physics.

The autopilot is four checkboxes in the controls panel, each also on a key: `X` chases the predicted landing, `shift`+`X` takes the timed intercept that arrives with the ball instead of rushing and braking, `Z` keeps the drive armed only while the passive bounce would fall short of a full strike on the rock — a passive apex one pulse of height past the ceiling line, so the ball meets the face with real speed instead of a bare touch — and releases a caught ball, and `C` slews the tilt at 45°/s so the next reflection leaves straight up. A green status line reports one clause per engaged duty, naming the intercept mark and time, the pulse state and the wanted tilt. Every duty yields to the manual command that does the same thing — `A`/`D` or a travel click takes the intercept back, `Space` takes the drive back, and the slider, wheel or drag takes the tilt back — without unticking its checkbox. Held movement and tilt controls keep that duty suspended; it resumes after two seconds of idle simulation time. Travel clicks, wheel/slider adjustments and drive toggles use the same two-second grace period. The status line says “manual override” while suspended. Only the checkbox or autopilot shortcut permanently switches a duty off. The chase centres the reflector face on the landing, the timed intercept brakes recuperatively when the flywheel is installed, and the level bounce respects the piston's slew rate and range. Once **Trajectory computer II** is researched, both intercept switches use the economical timed controller. The computer looks through rock strikes to the next deck/ground landing, so travel can begin while the ball is still ascending instead of waiting for its fall; every real bounce immediately produces a new landing and deadline. The drawn overlay stops at the second ground, deck or rock contact — or at the first when that contact ends the flight — while the autopilot prices its own longer forecast in a separate trace; its marker and the autopilot always identify the same next actionable landing rather than mistaking a rock strike for a destination. With more than one ball the clause names the one being worked (`ball 1/2`) and drops the promise when the landing is out of reach, turning the run into the pickup that follows the grounding. The intercept prices every ball the rig may push by the time it can be under control, so a landing it cannot reach never takes the plan from one it can, and a ball already on the floor is the plan whenever no landing can be met. Measurements: `experiments/logs/autopilot.1-trips.txt` and `autopilot.2-trips.txt` for the previous chamber tunings, `autopilot.3-trips.txt` for two shared balls.

The chamber is 24 × 15 m (1.5× its previous height), with the 3.625 m rock layer at 11.375–15 m (grown from 2 m) so the extra space is open flight room. The chamber is drawn with a vertical perspective: true size up to the near edge of the rock layer at 11.375 m, then compressed to 0.25× (4× smaller) at the roof on a monotone cubic, giving a receding, logarithmic feel. Fallen debris and the platforms are below the warp and keep their true size. `PERSPECTIVE` decides whether the ball obeys the same law, and it is a real body either way: under `Depth scale` the ball's radius is the chamber's own local scale at its height — the radius it is drawn at, the clearance it keeps to a wall or the roof, the height it rests on a deck and the path the ballistic computer flies are all that one number, so a ball up in the layer is a smaller ball in a shrunken-looking layer instead of a full-size ball in front of small cells. Below the layer the scale is 1×, so the floor game is identical under either setting; `Constant size` is the classic 0.18 m sphere everywhere. The setting is a chamber rule and survives New game. A strike footprint is measured in cells, not in ball radii, so it does not shrink with the ball: `F4` (or the help drawer's perspective topic) rings every ball with its owner's real damage radius, foreshortened by the chamber's local scale so the ring lies on the ball's own plane. The ball is another 1.2× slower: its total flight scale is now 1 / 1.8, effective inertia 3.24 kg and ball-specific gravity 9.81 / 3.24 m/s². Inertia and gravity are scaled together to preserve the energy budget and bounce heights; debris still falls at 9.81 m/s². Engines come in tiers: tier 1 is 480 N / 12 m/s, traction research and motor machinery raise force and top speed through the same delta channels as every other upgrade, and the full chain reaches 1920 N / 30 m/s (4× tier-1 force) — enough at 100% force to meet a far-side fall that tier 1 cannot, paid for in reserve draw and braking heat. A 12 kJ reserve and the centre charge pad make recharging part of normal play.

The rock layer is a seeded 192 × 29 two-dimensional field. Its 0.125 m cells project to approximately 4 × 4 px at the reference scale. By default, the exposed rock boundary is rendered and collided as a smooth heightmap polyline so the ball reflects at continuous slope angles; an optional square-cell mode collides against exposed faces and rounded corners. Rock reflection keeps the ball's impulse: each contact applies at most one reflection impulse per step (never amplifying speed), and a ball that overlaps solid rock — for example right after switching rock shape — is separated back to contact distance instead of flying through at full speed. Impact loss applies 2× hit-point strength removal falling linearly to zero over a 8.78-cell (2.93×) radius with a fast power-2.8 falloff (1–2 cells typically destroyed, medium damage mid-ring, slight damage wide). A blow never clears its whole radius: it spends at most half of a cell's remaining strength, so the contact cells stay standing and the ring around them is left loose. The ceiling gives way a region at a time on a slow clock, with a probability that rises with how battered the region is. Only the rock's free face can let go: a blow that would finish a cell with rock still under it leaves it standing shattered at a held share of its strength until the face above it is worked away. A successful roll grows a clump off that face from an exposed seed. Growth is a mixing weight, not a threshold: the rock's element field is smooth, so at the slider floor a release is the plain clump of loose free faces the rock always gave, and rising growth turns more of the piece into a connected, mineral-matched flake until every cell follows that rule. The dominant mineral sets the setting (each row's `growth` in `js/minerals.js` is the default, from Stone 120% down to Gas-bearing rock 20%) and proportionally reduces the release chance, trading frequent grains for rarer, heavier falls. Sliders tune each mineral live from 10% to 500%. The rock carries three independent element concentrations, each its own seeded noise field compressed in Y so deposits read as flat seams rather than round patches. The material table defines each mineral as a feathered range per element; a cell's normalised memberships are its mineral mix, so a narrow range is a rare mineral and overlapping ranges blend. One table in `js/minerals.js` holds every material: a stone host plus Iron ore, Copper ore, Quartz stone, Corundum, Fluorite, Native silver, Gold-bearing quartz and Gas-bearing rock, and the same row supplies its colour, impact strength in J/cell, base-price demand cycle and default fall grouping. Generate test rock starts a fresh run with one pure band per mineral from left to right and a looseness gradient from 0% at the top to 100% at the bottom; the Damage rock view shows the gradient. Loose cells are drawn as a hairline on the exposed face, and the inspector marks a cell loose when its damage is high enough to let it go. A released group is not redrawn as one block: each cell falls as its own 0.6 kg grain at its own cell size and position, so a piece keeps the shape it broke out in, lands in the columns it came from and raises the fallen-debris heightmap under each of them. Grains just let go still hang at the face they broke from, so they are drawn under the lowest ceiling their own width spans and never as rubble inside the wall behind them. All failed and detached cells become falling fragments with no pool-exhaustion losses. Fragments that reach the floor build a fallen-debris heightmap with 192 columns. Each soil tile is 0.125 m wide by 0.03125 m tall (`w:h = 4:1`) by default; the interpolated top profile stays smooth for ball contact and platform travel. `DEBRIS` in the system bar compresses that height from 1 (square rubble) to 8 (a flat sheet, 0.015625 m per layer) in half steps. It is a chamber rule, kept across New game: the deposit, garage grader, road-grader blade, floor harvest and the tile drawing all convert cells to metres through the same layer thickness, so a pile holds the same material and value at every setting. The number is the ground, not only the picture: changing it re-lays the rubble already on the floor by the same ratio, so the surface the ball and the platform ride moves with what is drawn. A pile may stand as high as the roof — the chamber's ceiling is the only level a column cannot pass — so enough fallen rock can bury the whole flight room; a setting that would push a column through the roof keeps what fits and books the rest as lost. A very deep column is charted in at most 64 rows of proportionally taller tiles, so a pile at the roof costs no more to draw than a shallow one. The platform follows the ground under its **wheels**, pitching gradually to the terrain instead of hovering on a fixed deck. The wheels sit at the hull's ends, so a wheel — not the corner of a flat keel — meets a 45° plate first and the machine climbs on its wheels; the underside between them is a shallow arch that only comes into play once a corner is all the way in under the hull. The road grader works a 1.5 m swath under the platform centre while moving (not ahead), shaving and smoothing the top; cuts clamp at the original y = 0 floor, so it never creates pits and the terrain slope remains gentle. The blade does not destroy what it shaves: every column keeps its value density, so the material the edge removed becomes the blade's own claim, settled as one `blade` ledger entry (50% of the shaved value) after the platform stops finding rubble for a quarter second. Working adds rolling resistance that slows the platform and dissipates as heat. The garage grader's low dome is deliberately **not** terrain — it is 0.12 m tall against the 0.2 m belly, so the hull passes over it and the wheels never lift the deck out from under a ball.

Storage holds 100 cells. A full 60 kg load equals the dry platform mass, reducing acceleration and participating in capture momentum, braking, and kinetic-energy accounting. Touch either wall marked **SELL** to sell the entire load automatically. The MARKET / LEDGER drawer breaks cargo and lifetime sales down by mineral (St/Fe/Cu/Si/Al/Fl/Ag/Au/Gb) and records each wall sale in a ledger with time, mass, mix, and credits. Its cr/cell column is the live market: every run opens with seed-random demand, each mineral's quote drifts on its own slow cycle (115–480 s at 1×), and base prices follow the abundance measured over the run's opening bed on a rarer-is-dearer curve, so a rare ore is dear and a common one can still spike. Upper rows generated as the rock scrolls do not reprice that market, so the calibration population stays the bed the run started in. A broken fragment is booked at the price of the moment it breaks, which keeps cargo, rubble and the ledger exact while the market moves. Purchases, research and recharges debit the same funds ledger, and a garage-grader sweep or a road-grader blade stint adds its share back as its own free-form entry. The drawer also sells energy back into the reserve at 25 cr/kJ up to the 12 kJ capacity, buying only what the current funds cover; imported energy is tracked as `importedEnergy` and subtracted from the balance, mirroring the exported terms, so trading in either direction keeps the ledger exact. The telemetry strip is a full-width row under the system bar, reporting the reserve, cargo, total moving platform mass, funds, ball energy, stored thermal energy and temperature, and an energy balance that includes attached rock, debris, cargo, exported material, and imported fuel. Warnings highlight the card that needs attention instead of adding readouts: the reserve card tags `LOW` below 10% and `EMPTY` under 0.5 J, the cargo card tags `FULL` at the 100-cell limit, and the temperature card tags `HOT` while the drive is derated and `CUTOFF` when powered output stops. Each warning note says what to do — park on the pad, sell at a wall, or wait for cooling.

The selected player's equipment panel sells upgrades against that player's measured sale income: a road grader (needs no research, 300 cr, +10 kg, smooths and shaves a 1.5 m swath under the centre while moving and adds rolling resistance when working), a rebound pad (+3% normal-energy retention), a drive coil (+12 J bounce pulse), a high-torque motor (+360 N engine force, +6 m/s top speed, +6 kg net), an overdrive unit (+480 N, +6 m/s, +8 kg), a lightweight chassis (−12 kg dry mass), a radiator family (vent / array / heavy bank, +0.15–0.70 m² cooling, +4–16 kg), ball upgrades (hardened steel +15% fracture/14% rock loss/8% wider splash/+3 kg, spiked hammer +35%/20%/16%/+5 kg — higher damage but more bounce energy lost on rock, fewer bounces per charge), and a brake flywheel (returns 55% of braking energy to the reserve at 90% round-trip efficiency, +14 kg). Paid upgrades name the research node that unlocks them — the road grader needs none — so purchases are refused without funds, without the prerequisite research, or when already installed. The road grader is the rig's own tool: a competitor's chassis already harvests the rubble it drives over as part of its policy, so every rival's row reads `Locked` and the refusal says so rather than naming a prerequisite the item does not have. Radiator and ball tiers share one installed unit each: fitting a tier swaps the previous one together with its mass and cooling/damage deltas. Installed items add explicit deltas to their owner's machine only, and installation mass changes preserve the energy ledger. An installed-specification table shows the live dry mass, engine force, top speed, bounce pulse, platform retention, rock retention, rock damage, damage radius, convective cooling, and brake recovery that all research and equipment deltas currently produce, which is what each row's stated effect compares against.

A **competitor platform** shares the chamber with the rig. Every player starts with the same stock 3.6 m chassis, deck, wheels, tail plates and electric engine, but their machines diverge through play. Each has its own purse, research tree, equipment ownership, engine bay, radiator and strike-ball tier. Selecting a player makes SCIENCE + SHOP operate on that profile: research and purchases debit only its sale proceeds and enter its own frozen purchase ledger, while its force, speed, mass, cooling, retention, pulse and damage statistics change without touching the rig or another rival. A bot competitor automatically invests each wall settlement in a deterministic build order, leaves its ramp installation alone, and saves what it cannot yet spend; gross sales remain the sum of its current purse and lifetime spending. The order buys only what the competitor's own policy reads — a stronger pulse, a stronger drive, then the mass cuts, and the forecast chain last — because the page overlay, the cooling chain, the strike-ball metallurgy and the brake flywheel change nothing a rival's keeper or drive ever consults.

The competitor plays with its resulting machine. It meets balls it may push, makes short rubble sweeps while a flight is safely away, returns before contact, scoops grounded balls, prices the reflected apex against the same strike line the rig’s keeper uses to decide whether to pulse, and bisects its own reflector tilt with the chamber’s contact solver so the next bounce leaves vertically. Manual travel, tilt and drive commands temporarily yield the corresponding keeper back to the player. An owned ball uses its owner’s strike-head mass, damage and rock-retention tier; a shared ball keeps the rig’s ball specification. A ball the competitor may not push is outside its policy, and competitor balls, catches and pulses remain outside the rig’s energy ledger.

All platforms share one drawing routine and palette: the selected platform is drawn whole and every other platform is that same complete drawing at 60% alpha, set fresh on every frame so repeated switching can never compound the dim. Each hull carries the whole machine, with ramps drawn only while installed, its fuel bar, thermometer, collector strip and reflector piston included, so a competitor is never a stripped-down second vehicle. The wheels sit at the hull's ends and the underside arches up between them, so what meets a ramp, a rock shoulder or a deck edge is a wheel and not a flat keel corner. Each wheel hangs on a strut of 0.24 m: the hull's pose is still the wheel line and the arch, and a strut only follows the ground down, so a machine cresting another hull or coming down a 0.5 m step keeps its wheels on the ground instead of perching on its belly with them in the air. The struts are slack on level ground and the drawing shows one only once a wheel has dropped clear of the hull line.

Every player starts with **Ramp** installed in the base-chassis shop. It is free to uninstall and reinstall anywhere, for the selected player only; the stock hardware is already included in chassis mass. Bots never change this choice, and New game restores it. **Ride-over requires a ramp on every player**: one missing ramp disables climbing for the whole chamber, even between two players that still have theirs. Installed plates fold away and all platforms push instead. The shop distinguishes an installed-but-inactive ramp from a removed one.

With all ramps installed, the strips of the body at either end are **retractable 45° plates**. A plate stays folded — a plain vertical end face — while the nearest platform on that side is beyond its reach or moving away; as the gap closes it swings down at 90°/s and reaches the floor at 45° when the platforms are within 0.6 m. That reach belongs to the closing speed: one swing takes half a second and a hull under drive is still accelerating over the distance, so the ramp starts down two swings out — a crawling approach keeps the standing 2.6 m, one arriving at 12 m/s is met from 12.6 m and still finds a full 45° wedge. A plate holds while any part of the other hull is over it and folds only once that hull's near edge has passed the ramp's tip, so a wheel leaving the deck is never left on a ledge. A hull riding on the deck brings **both** plates down at once, and so does a hull closing fast enough to cross that deck inside one swing, because by the time it is up there the far plate's own swing is over: either way the rider drives off a ramp rather than dropping off a ledge. The plate is clipped at the floor, so the line the wheels follow is the line the drawing shows: a shorter, steeper ramp when it is half open, the full 45° wedge when it is down. Measurements: `experiments/logs/players.10-plate.txt` for the hull, wheels and struts over plates and stairs, `players.10-ramp.txt` for the plate angle every wheel meets.

**With ramps, which platform is on top starts with drive power**, not by who is the rig: the platform commanding movement with the higher force setting (its own slider value) begins the climb, and a coasting or braking platform applies none. Ties keep the rig up, which makes an idle pair stable. Once one hull is above the other, the pair keeps that rider until the bodies part and the tails fold away: a brake, a mark reached or a direction change mid-crossing cannot swap the two hulls and make them stutter through each other. The ground query is strictly layered — a platform only sees the bodies it outranks, one way only — so the platform on top climbs the other's plate and deck, the one underneath stays on the floor instead of being lifted by a body above it, and no pair can lift itself step by step.

**Without ramps**, horizontal bumpers prevent platforms from passing through one another. Inelastic contacts share momentum using each machine’s actual mass, including cargo, machinery and a held ball. **B** holds a mechanical brake at `1.2 × mass × gravity`, independent of throttle, engine, temperature and fuel. A stock 60 kg brake resists about 706 N versus 480 N of full stock thrust: slow pushing cannot creep it along, but a fast impact exceeds its finite impulse budget and makes it slide until friction stops it. An 8 m/s coasting stock platform shoves an equal-mass braked one about 1.35 m; stronger upgraded engines can also overcome the brake. The contact solve handles both walls and trains of platforms before balls meet their decks. Collision losses become heat; a flywheel only recuperates actual brake work. Signed `platformWork` accounts for energy transferred to or from competitors outside the rig ledger, keeping the displayed balance exact.

The **ball arrangement** is the system-bar setting beside the rock shape. `1 shared` is the game above: one ball either platform may push. `2 shared` puts two shared balls in flight, and every platform still holds one at a time — a second ball lands on the collector strip and bounces until the first is released. The ball readout works the **selected** platform's ball: the one it holds, else the one landing soonest, so the page always describes the platform under the controls (`· 2 balls` marks a machine with more than one ball it may push). The ballistic computer draws a flight for every ball that machine may push — the focused one with its landing mark and coasting ghost, the others as dimmer dotted paths — and with `1 per platform` it never draws another platform's owned ball. The autopilot works the rig's focused ball: the held one, else the one the intercept chose, else the one landing soonest, so two balls never split the plan. The autopilot's choice is the ball it can bring under control soonest of those it can still keep in play — a landing it can meet beats any pickup, a ball on the floor beats a landing it cannot reach — and it stays with that ball until another offers control clearly sooner. `1 per platform` gives the rig and every competitor a ball of its own, and ownership becomes the contact rule: `mayPush` is checked before any collector, piston or deck query, so a platform does not see a ball it may not push — it passes through, books nothing in that platform's ledger, and balls never collide with each other. A competitor works its ball with the rig's own machinery: its collector catches, its reflector bounces off the competitor's own tilt with the shared impulse solver, a pulse from the competitor's own machinery is added while the reflected apex would still fall short of a full strike on the rock (the same one-pulse strike line as the rig’s keeper, sized off that competitor’s own pulse), and a grounded ball is scooped back onto its deck by driving over it — the collector also holds a caught ball until the bot releases it, so the same ball mines the ceiling and the rubble lands for its chassis to shave. A competitor's ball, catches and pulses stay outside the rig's balance, exactly like the commercial grader's blade; a shared ball a competitor catches books the transfer, and its release books the gift as imported work.

The PLAYERS strip is the roster. Clicking a line makes that platform active and moves the whole view to it: `A`/`D`, the brake, the **force slider** (each player owns its setting, and the slider reads back the selected player's value), the low-ground travel click, and the motion readout. Because every platform is the same machine, the menu is the same menu: the panel title names the platform and the policy it runs under (`YOU · MANUAL`, `RIVAL 1 · BOT`), and the bounce drive, the release and the piston tilt stay on screen and act for the selected platform; the clearly labelled RIG AUTOPILOT checkboxes always belong to the rig — nothing appears or disappears with the switch. The ballistic computer and the ball readout follow the same selection, and a caught ball is released by the machine that holds it: a competitor's release spends its own pulse and leaves the rig's reserve untouched. The keys always address the selected platform, so a direction key held during a switch is dropped with the command it gave, and pressing it again drives only the platform in the title: neither platform can move on a key pressed for the other. Taking a competitor's controls does not silence its policy; only a key, a brake, a travel click or its own `BOT` switch does. The telemetry cards describe the machine and do not change with the selection. The `BOT` checkbox hands a platform to its policy. A bot competitor plays the ball with its own drive and tilt keepers, banks what its chassis shaves, sells a full hopper at the nearer wall and invests the proceeds in its own upgrades; a bot rig enables the autopilot's intercept, drive-keeper and level-bounce duties and, whenever its hopper is full, makes a selling trip to the nearer wall — suspending the intercept duty through its own override while the drive keeper keeps the ball in play — before resuming the intercept. A hand travel, tilt or drive command suspends the corresponding competitor policy for the same two seconds the autopilot's overrides use, so taking the controls never fights the bot.

The separate **commercial grader** is a call/dismiss service in the base-chassis root of the SCIENCE + SHOP tree. Its persistent low vehicle starts parked inside a recessed 0.25 m-high garage outside the right wall. Calling sends it out to grade wall-to-wall sweeps for a 50% share; dismissing stops the blade and drives the same vehicle home with its current load, which is settled on arrival. It remains drawn while parked, working and returning, and calling during a return resumes work from its current position instead of respawning it.

The SCIENCE + SHOP drawer renders a single visual progression tree with SVG prerequisite edges, hover path highlighting, and locked / available / researched node cards. Each player owns a separate tree, purse and set of installed tiers; selecting a player edits only that profile: rebound materials (+1% retention, 180 cr, unlocks rebound pad), light alloys (−4 kg dry mass, 240 cr, unlocks lightweight chassis), pulse electromagnetics (+4 J pulse, 300 cr, needs rebound materials, unlocks drive coil), traction motors (+240 N, +3 m/s, 380 cr, needs pulse electromagnetics, unlocks high-torque motor), heat transfer (+0.5 W/K convective cooling, 340 cr, needs light alloys, unlocks cooling radiator tiers), flywheel storage (460 cr, needs heat transfer, unlocks brake flywheel), power electronics (+360 N, +3 m/s, 640 cr, needs traction motors and heat transfer, unlocks overdrive unit), impact forging (320 cr, needs rebound materials, unlocks hardened and spiked strike balls), ballistic computer (520 cr, needs pulse electromagnetics, activates the trajectory overlay), and trajectory computer II (700 cr, needs ballistic computer and power electronics, activates 3-bounce forecasting and early economy intercepts). Each node changes a measured simulation quantity rather than acting as a label, and research mass changes couple to the moving platform exactly like equipment installations. The tree and shop together cost roughly 8.5 k cr with ball and radiator families; a pad-aware scripted shift grosses roughly 200–400 cr over 240 s, so completing both is a multi-shift investment; the current calibration lives in `experiments/logs/engine.1-trips.txt`.

Researching the ballistic computer for a player unlocks that player's predicted flights over the chamber: one dotted passive trajectory per ball the selected machine may push, each following wall, ceiling, ground, deck and rock reflections with the simulation's own gravity and retention, the focus flight carrying a landing mark and a platform ghost coasting to the predicted landing time. A machine without its own computer has no page overlay, even when another player owns one. The computer is the **selected** platform's: it simulates that machine's collector and reflector (its own tilt and drive) against the ball that platform may push, and its readout, ghost and release address it. A collector that already holds a ball returns the next arrival off its flat strip, and a forecast through a tilted reflector uses the chamber's own impulse solver — friction, recoil and pulse included — so the second contact it draws is the contact that really happens. The autopilot prices its own plans at the same time in a second, separate trace, so the rig's plan can never replace the flight the player is looking at — with the rig selected as well as with a competitor. A drawn path stops at the second bounce, at the first contact when that strike absorbs all speed, or at the first rock contact of a tier-I computer; a horizon derived from chamber height and ball gravity (about 7.3 seconds at the current tuning) bounds a flight with no contact. It is flagged uncertain rather than promising a rebound through damaged rock, and the status line reports whether the intercept is reachable at full thrust or how many metres short it falls. Prediction reads the world without mutating it and allocates nothing per call, so it runs from the readout cadence into preallocated buffers.

The readout cadence only rewrites what changed. Telemetry values (clock, reserve, energies, temperature, motion) update freely, but buttons, status lines, panel notes, specifications, inventory and control state go through change-only writers: an unchanged string, `aria-pressed`/`aria-disabled` value, row state, slider position or disabled flag is never assigned again, so a settled or paused page performs no redundant DOM writes outside telemetry.

Energy no longer has to be bought to be used. A 4 m strip of ground at the chamber centre is a charge pad: while the platform stands still on it (`|vx| < 0.1 m/s`), the installed engine draws 3 kW, filling the 12 kJ reserve in exactly 4 s. The pad is a ground type rather than a consumable or a purchase, so the cost of energy is the trip to the middle and the time spent stopped; the import is booked like a bought recharge so the energy ledger stays exact. The market recharge remains as an emergency resupply at 25 cr/kJ when the platform cannot wait for the pad. And the pad is always reachable: on an empty reserve the crew hand-cranks the platform at 60 N up to a 0.8 m/s crawl — movement only, booked as imported crew work — so a run can always crawl to the centre or to a selling wall instead of soft-locking.

Chamber interaction is split by height: hovering visible rock shows that cell’s tooltip (column, layer, remaining strength, mineral mix, price and elemental composition), and clicking the low band (up to the marked 1.7 m target height) commands travel. Open air issues neither command, so inspecting rock never moves the platform. Rock-colour modes stay visible beneath the chamber; clicking them changes only display, not physics.

The platform is asymmetric: only the right-hand reflector bounces, so the left wall has a pocket that the collector docks into so the reflector can cover the wall all the way to `x = 0`. Docking trades catching (the hidden collector cannot catch) for full wall coverage when bouncing. To catch debris near the wall, undock so the collector is exposed.

A deck's pose is prescribed by the terrain under it, so a boulder shoulder can lift a platform by more in one step than any rate limit allows; that jump is a pose correction rather than motion, and a contact solve sees the deck moving at most at the platform's own limits (its top speed, its 3 m/s terrain drop rate and its 90°/s pitch rate). Passive deck/rock impacts retain 90% of normal kinetic energy. Platform/ball horizontal contact friction exchanges momentum and energy, and capture/release carries platform velocity. Side walls are ideal elastic boundaries for the ball. Stored thermal energy maps to temperature using a 300 J/K machinery heat capacity above 20 °C. The model removes heat through convection and Stefan–Boltzmann radiation and tracks cooling as exported energy in the balance. Powered movement and bounce output derate linearly from 85 °C to a 135 °C cutoff; braking remains available. The radiator array adds real exposed area to both cooling paths.

`SteamCeiling.state` is a stable live view: inspect scalar values as properties (`SteamCeiling.state.funds`, `.cargoMass`, `.airborneCells`) without creating snapshot objects or calling query-like methods. Commands such as `damage`, `sellCargo`, `purchase`, `recharge`, `setShape`, and `reset` remain methods; simulation buffers are exposed as typed arrays for inspection and deterministic tests.
